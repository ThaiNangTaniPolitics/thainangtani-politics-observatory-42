## 0029 – Historical Development of ISOC
### *From Counter-Communism to a Statutory Security Agency*

*Last updated: 9 October 2026, 04:29:06 (ICT)*

The Internal Security Operations Command (ISOC, กอ.รมน.) was created as a counter-insurgency command in 1965. It survived the end of the communist insurgency, was carried through three decades by administrative orders, and became a statutory agency in 2008. This node traces that path from documented sources. Its interpretation as the back-end of a dual governance structure is set out in 0037.

*Correction (October 2026): an earlier version of this node carried no sources. It stated that the Internal Security Act of 2008 granted ISOC "operational immunity" and "the ability to bypass administrative courts", that ISOC created the Village Scouts, and that "007" was an intelligence network. The sources do not support these statements (Sections 3 and 4). The node has been rebuilt.*

## 1. Scope and Method

The main source is Puangthong Pawakapan, *Infiltrating Society: The Thai Military's Internal Security Affairs* (ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute, 2021), read in the original; page numbers are the printed pages (Level 2). Statutory text is cited from the Royal Gazette (Level 1). The documentary basis for ISOC's statutory mandate and the corrections on legal shielding are set out in detail in 0035.

-----

## 2. Origins: Counter-Insurgency and the Political Offensive (1965–1988)

- **Creation.** The Communist Suppression Operations Command (CSOC) was created on 14 December 1965 and *"renamed as the Internal Security Operations Command (ISOC) in 1974"* (Puangthong, p. 24). Its authority rested on an order by General Praphas Charusathien, who appointed himself director (p. 55).
- **External support.** Puangthong: *"the support and influence of the United States were crucial for the Thai military's adoption of modern counterinsurgency operations"* (p. 20).
- **Civilian-police-military operations.** ISOC's counter-insurgency doctrine joined civil servants, police, military and local people in "civilian-police-military joint operations" (CPM). The term "civilian" *"meant civil servants and the people, especially the locals living in the areas under the counterinsurgency"* (p. 26). Mass organisations were later built on the same concept (p. 92).
- **The political offensive.** Under General Prem Tinsulanonda as prime minister, Prime Ministerial Orders No. 66/2523 (1980) and No. 65/2525 (1982) replaced the hardline military offensive with a political approach and obliged all state agencies to comply. In Puangthong's assessment, the two orders *"significantly increased ISOC's power"* (p. 43). Order No. 83/2526 (1983) placed civilian, military and police agencies under ISOC's command, together with mass organisations such as the Volunteer Defence Corps (p. 43). Details in 0035, Section 2.

-----

## 3. Survival by Administrative Order and Statutory Basis (1992–2008)

- **Mission broadening after 1992.** After the violent suppression of protests in May 1992, elected governments broadened the military's non-military role. Prime Minister Chuan Leekpai announced support for the military's participation in *"economic development, public health provisions, disaster relief operations, protection of natural resources and solving environmental problems"* (Puangthong, p. 45). In the late 1990s the concept of "non-traditional security threats" — terrorism, drug trafficking, human trafficking, migration, disease, environmental problems — entered Thai security thinking. Puangthong: *"The military was happy to adopt these new security threats into its mission"* (p. 46).
- **Survival.** On 1 April 2000 the Chuan cabinet repealed the Anti-Communist Activities Act. A month earlier, Prime Ministerial Order No. 187/2542 had extended ISOC's life, and ISOC was given responsibility for coordinating operations against narcotics (pp. 45, 55). In May 2002, Prime Minister Thaksin Shinawatra's Order No. 158/2545 extended ISOC's anti-drug responsibility and changed its structure (p. 55).
- **Dependence on orders.** Puangthong concludes that *"ISOC's existence was always dependent on administrative orders, and that a government could always change or terminate ISOC by simply issuing a Prime Ministerial Order"* (p. 55).
- **Statutory basis.** The Internal Security Act B.E. 2551 (2008), prepared under the post-coup Surayud government and in force since 28 February 2008, *"for the first time constituted ISOC by legislation"* (p. 55). Under the Act, ISOC is a special government agency in the Office of the Prime Minister under the Prime Minister's direct command (§ 5), and a government agency under the law on budget procedures (§ 6).

**What the Act does not provide.** The earlier version of this node attributed "operational immunity" and "the ability to bypass administrative courts" to the Act. The Act contains no immunity clause; section 20 obliges ISOC to compensate innocent persons for losses caused by its special powers. Section 23 assigns cases arising from Chapter 2 measures — the special powers that require a cabinet resolution for a defined area and period — to the ordinary courts rather than the administrative courts. The exemption from civil, criminal and disciplinary liability is found in section 17 of the Emergency Decree B.E. 2548 (2005), not in the 2008 Act. Wording and sources: 0035, Section 5.

-----

## 4. Mass Organisations

- **Thai National Defence Volunteers (TNDV).** Established by the military government of General Kriangsak Chamanan in 1978 as an umbrella for village militia groups; *"TNDV has been under the direct authority of ISOC since its establishment"* (Puangthong, p. 98). According to an ISOC progress report of 2012, its activities declined from 1992 and were revived in 2006 (p. 4, citing ISOC 2012).
- **Village Scouts.** The Village Scouts *"were founded in 1971 under the BPP"* — the Border Patrol Police — not under ISOC (p. 101).
- **"007".** The "007 Security News Line" is one of the hotline accounts ISOC created on Facebook, Line and Twitter *"for people to report undesirable political activities"* (p. 130). It is a reporting channel, not an intelligence network.
- **Size.** ISOC's organised mass numbered 236,419 by Puangthong's count from ISOC's website (p. 128; the table heading dates the figure to March 2012, the text to March 2015).

The revival of mass organisations since 2006 and its ideological content are documented in 0035, Section 6.

-----

## 5. Scope of "Internal Security" Today

Puangthong summarises ISOC's own publications: *"ISOC's current conception of national security has expanded to cover the affairs of minorities and illegal migrants, drug trafficking, cybercrimes, terrorism, deforestation, conflict over natural resources, 'influential people' and mafia gangs, and even natural disasters"* (p. 40). ISOC's strategy for 2017–2021 devotes one of its five strategic issues to the southern border provinces (strategic issue 4; ISOC Strategy 2017–2021, p. 2).

-----

## 6. Findings and Limits

**Documented:**
- ISOC began as a counter-insurgency command in 1965.
- Its powers were extended by prime ministerial orders under Prem (1980–1986), Chuan (2000) and Thaksin (2002).
- It acquired a statutory basis only in 2008.
- After 1992, elected governments broadened its mission to non-military fields.
- It directs a mass organisation (TNDV) that has been under its authority since 1978.

**Corrected:** The 2008 Act does not grant immunity. The administrative-court rule is limited to Chapter 2 measures. The Village Scouts were a Border Patrol Police creation. "007" is a reporting hotline.

**Interpretation:** that this trajectory makes ISOC the back-end of a dual governance structure (0037).

**Open:** ISOC's budget in the southern border provinces and the dispute over its "other expenditure" share, to be documented from primary budget documents (0034). The earlier statement that "multiple research studies confirm" special treatment of these budget lines is withdrawn for want of a source.
