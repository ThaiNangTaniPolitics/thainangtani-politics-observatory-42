## 0075 – The Compression: Thailand's Prisons 2021–2026, the Capacity Standard That Defines Away the Crowding, and What Grew While the Total Fell
### *How a 1.6 m² benchmark makes a 21% overcrowding figure out of a system holding people at roughly 1.3 m² each — and why the population that grew fastest is the one that has not been convicted*

---

## 1. Scope and the claim

This node examines Thailand's prison population as reported for the year January–December 2025, and asks two questions that the headline figures obscure.

The first is instrumental: **Thailand's stated capacity of 248,330 places rests on an allocation of 1.6 m² per inmate.** Overcrowding is then expressed as a percentage above that number. The benchmark is doing the work. A capacity standard set at the physical floor produces a modest-sounding overcrowding figure for a system that is, in fact, holding people in roughly the space of a doorway.

The second is directional: **the total prison population fell by 11% between 2021 and 2025, but pre-trial detention rose by 28% over the same period, and pre-trial detention of women rose by 61%.** The system contracted at the sentenced end and expanded at the end where no conviction exists. In 2025 the total reversed and rose again by 7%.

The node makes no claim that any individual is wrongly held, and no claim of intent behind either pattern. It claims that the measure and the direction are both documented, and that the second is invisible if only the first is reported.

This is the same analytical shape as [0074](0074-the-uncounted-debt-and-the-poverty-line.md), where a poverty line of 3,043 baht produced a poverty count and the eligibility filter measured assets rather than need. There, the instrument defined the problem down. Here, the capacity standard does.

---

## 2. The source, and why this one

**FIDH – Thailand Annual Prison Report 2026, N°853a, March 2026**, covering January to December 2025.

The choice of source is itself a finding. The two most cited alternatives are institutionally bound: the **Thailand Institute of Justice (TIJ)** is a Thai state-adjacent body, and **UNODC** operates under the diplomatic constraints of member-state cooperation. Both produce reliable technical work; neither is positioned to characterise a national system as failing. FIDH is an international federation of human rights organisations with no such dependency, and it publishes the underlying figures rather than only conclusions drawn from them.

Where the Department of Corrections (DoC) is the origin of a number, this node says so. The 1.6 m² allocation, in particular, is the DoC's own planning standard, not FIDH's characterisation of it.

## 3. The capacity standard as instrument

**Content analysis**

| Measure | Value (Dec 2025) |
|---|---|
| Total prison population | **301,020** (262,784 men / 38,236 women) |
| Stated capacity | **248,330** |
| Space per inmate in the capacity calculation | **1.6 m²** (DoC standard) |
| Overcrowding, system-wide | **21%** above capacity |
| Overcrowding, men | +23% |
| Overcrowding, women | +9% |
| Prisons above capacity | **120 of 143 (84%)** |

The trajectory of the last figure is the one to hold: **106 of 143 prisons above capacity in 2022 (74%), 102 in 2024 (71%), 120 in 2026 (84%).** The improvement between 2022 and 2024 was real and has been given back with interest.

**Derived figure — stated as a derivation, not as a reported one**

If the stated capacity of 248,330 places is constructed at 1.6 m² per place, the total accommodation implied is about **397,300 m²**. Distributed across the 301,020 people actually held, that is approximately **1.32 m² per person** — the footprint of a single bed with no floor left over.

*(This is arithmetic performed here on two FIDH figures. It is not a FIDH statement, and it assumes the capacity figure is constructed uniformly at the stated allocation. It should be presented as a derivation wherever it is used.)*

**Legal analysis**

The Nelson Mandela Rules (UN Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners, 2015) require accommodation meeting requirements of health, with attention to cubic content of air and floor space, but set no numeric floor. Numeric benchmarks come from elsewhere: the ICRC's habitat guidance and the European CPT standard both operate well above the Thai figure. **The relevant comparison must be verified against the primary documents before use** (see §10).

**Tension**

A capacity standard is normally a statement about what a system is designed to hold. Here it functions as a denominator. Set the allocation low enough and a severely crowded system reports as moderately crowded — without a single person being released and without the standard ever being defended in public.

## 4. The divergence: what fell and what grew

**Content analysis**

- **Total population, 2021–2025: −11%.**
- **In 2025 alone: +7%** (men +7%, women +10%).
- **Pre-trial detention, total: 30,428**, an increase of **28% since January 2021**.
- **Pre-trial detention, men: +24%.**
- **Pre-trial detention, women: 2,840 → 4,559**, an increase of **61%**.

Roughly **one in ten** people in Thai prisons — 30,428 of 301,020 — has not been convicted. Among women the proportion is higher: 4,559 of 38,236, about **one in eight**.

**Legal analysis**

Pre-trial detention is the point in a criminal process where the presumption of innocence is formally intact and materially suspended. Its growth is not a function of sentencing policy, court capacity, or crime rates alone; it is a function of bail practice — of how often release pending trial is granted, and on what security. A population that shrinks at the sentenced end while growing at the pre-trial end is not being decarcerated. It is being processed differently.

**Tension**

The 11% fall is the figure that circulates. It is true, and it describes a system that has meanwhile increased by more than a quarter the number of people it holds without a conviction. Both statements come from the same report.

## 5. Women as the leading indicator

**Content analysis**

- Women's population grew **+10% in 2025**, against men's +7%.
- Women's pre-trial detention grew **+61% since January 2021**, against men's +24%.
- Women held for drug offences: **23,724 of 38,236**, about **62%**.
- Women under sentence of death: **22 in January 2022 → 53** now, within a death-row total of **429** (376 men, 53 women). **98%** of the women there are held for drug offences, against 69% of the men.

**A note on what is deliberately not said here.** The change in the women's death-row figure is frequently expressed as a percentage. That percentage is large and the base is 22 people. This node gives the absolute numbers and declines the percentage, because a rate computed on a base that small measures the base, not the phenomenon. The same applies to any onward use of these figures.

**Legal analysis**

The **Bangkok Rules** — the UN Rules for the Treatment of Women Prisoners and Non-custodial Measures for Women Offenders, adopted by the General Assembly in 2010 — are the international instrument most directly applicable, and the one for which Thailand is most often credited (§8). Their central provision is a preference for non-custodial measures for women, particularly those who are primary caregivers and those whose offending is linked to poverty. Against that standard, a 61% rise in women's pre-trial detention is the specific indicator the instrument exists to suppress.

**Tension**

Thailand authored the international standard on women in prison and is, on its own reported figures, moving away from it faster for women than for men.

## 6. Buriram at 517%

**Content analysis**

**Buriram Provincial Prison stands at 517% of capacity — the most overcrowded in the country for the fourth consecutive year.** Roi Et follows at 221%, Krabi at 194%. The gap between first and second place is the finding: 517% is not the top of a distribution, it is an outlier by a factor of more than two.

At the stated 1.6 m² allocation, 517% of capacity corresponds to an occupancy density it is not useful to compute, because the figure ceases to describe accommodation.

**The observation, stated at its correct strength**

Buriram is the province at the centre of the Bhumjaithai patronage structures mapped elsewhere in this Observatory: the defence-appointment cohort in [0010](0010-buri‑ramization-of-defense.md), the Senate composition and the Khao Kradong file in [0061](0061-dsi-senate-investigation-silenced-under-bhumjaithai.md). The province has been the object of sustained political investment across several domains and, for four consecutive years, has held the country's worst prison without remedy.

**This is a statement about the distribution of attention, not about causation.** Nothing in the FIDH figures attributes the condition of Buriram Provincial Prison to any office-holder, and no such attribution is made here. Provincial prison populations are driven by regional court throughput and by the prison estate's fixed geography. What the record supports is narrower and still worth stating: the political investment that reaches this province does not reach its prison.

## 7. The drug-law reform, counted honestly

**Content analysis**

- Drug offences accounted for **81% of the prison population in January 2021**.
- They account for **70.3% now — 211,740 people**.

The share fell by more than ten points. Part of that fall is arithmetic: when a total shrinks, a dominant category's share can fall without the category itself shrinking. It is therefore necessary to check the absolute.

**Derived, with the working shown:** if the December 2025 total of 301,020 represents an 11% fall from January 2021, the earlier total was approximately **338,000**, of which 81% is approximately **274,000** held for drug offences. Against 211,740 now, that is a reduction on the order of **62,000 people, roughly 23%**.

**That reduction is real, and this node states it plainly.** The 2021 narcotics code reform and the subsequent reclassification of possession thresholds removed tens of thousands of people from Thai prisons. It is the single largest decarcerating measure in the period under review.

**Tension**

Two things are true at once. The reform worked, and the system has begun to refill: 2025 shows +7% overall, +10% for women, and the count of over-capacity prisons has moved from 102 to 120 in two years. A reform that reduces a stock without changing the flow produces exactly this shape — a step down followed by a resumed climb. The drug share at 70.3% also means the reform has not altered what Thai prisons are principally for.

## 8. The patronage frame

**Content analysis**

On 22 July 2026 the Bangkok Post published an opinion piece recalling the **Kamlangjai (Inspire) Project** and its role in permitting imprisoned mothers to keep and nurse infants up to the age of two, in establishing prison libraries, and in vocational programmes. The **Bangkok Rules** of 2010 are the associated international instrument, and Thailand's authorship of them is a matter of record.

Both are real. The Bangkok Rules are a substantive contribution to international standards and are invoked by prison monitors worldwide, including in the FIDH report that supplies the figures above.

**Analysis**

The question this node raises is not whether the reforms occurred but what their **legal character** is. A provision that arrives through patronage has three properties that a statutory entitlement does not: it is unenforceable by its beneficiaries, it is not uniform across the estate, and it terminates with the attention that sustains it. A woman in Thonburi whose infant remains with her holds no right she could assert if the arrangement were withdrawn.

Against that, the aggregate record for the same system: 120 of 143 institutions above capacity, a 61% rise in women held before trial, and a capacity standard of 1.6 m².

**Tension**

The reforms and the figures are not in contradiction; they operate at different scales. The reforms describe what the system can do where attention is directed. The figures describe what it does at scale, where attention is not. The analytical error is to read the first as evidence about the second — which is the move the commemorative frame invites.

## 9. What this node shares with 0074

Three instruments, one shape:

- **The poverty line** ([0074](0074-the-uncounted-debt-and-the-poverty-line.md)): set at 3,043 baht, it produces a poverty count that the state's own benefit schedule contradicts.
- **The welfare filter** ([0074](0074-the-uncounted-debt-and-the-poverty-line.md)): it measures credit records and vehicle ownership, and so identifies the visible rather than the poor.
- **The capacity standard** (here): set at 1.6 m², it produces an overcrowding figure that understates a system holding people at roughly 1.3 m².

In each case the instrument is technically correct, publicly available, and never defended — because it is never the subject of the argument. The number it yields is. This is the pattern catalogued as politically functional law in [0024](0024-politically-functional-law-and-technocratic-framing.md): a measure that performs a political function through a technical form.

The feeder relationship is also worth naming. [0069](0069-the-way-out-runs-through-the-politics.md) and [0074](0074-the-uncounted-debt-and-the-poverty-line.md) document household debt at levels where informal lending at 240% annual interest is a normal recourse. A drug-offence prison population of 211,740, disproportionately female and disproportionately poor, is not an unrelated file.

## 10. Discipline checklist (verification record)

- [x] **Every figure sourced to FIDH 2026, reporting year 2025.** Totals, capacity, per-prison figures, pre-trial figures, drug shares and death-row figures are as published.
- [x] **Derived figures marked as derived.** The 1.32 m² actual-density figure (§3) and the ~62,000 absolute reduction in drug imprisonment (§7) are arithmetic performed here, with the working shown. Neither is a FIDH statement.
- [x] **Percentages carry their absolute base.** 70.3% is given with 211,740; 62% with 23,724 of 38,236; 21% with 301,020 against 248,330.
- [x] **One percentage deliberately refused.** The women's death-row change is given as 22 → 53 within a total of 429, without a growth rate, because a base of 22 cannot carry one (§5).
- [x] **The reform credited.** §7 states plainly that the narcotics reform removed roughly 62,000 people from prison, rather than reporting only the share and implying stagnation.
- [x] **Buriram held at observational strength.** No causal claim, no attribution to any office-holder; the §112/patronage escalation is confined to an internal note.
- [ ] **TO VERIFY before any onward use:** the numeric international benchmarks in §3. The ICRC habitat guidance (commonly cited at 3.4 m² per person in shared accommodation) and the CPT standard (4 m² in multi-occupancy cells) are stated here from general knowledge and have **not** been checked against the primary documents. The Mandela Rules set no numeric floor, which is checked.
- [ ] **TO VERIFY:** whether the DoC 1.6 m² allocation is a current published planning standard or a figure reconstructed by FIDH from capacity and floor-area data. The distinction matters for §3, which is the node's principal instrumental claim.
