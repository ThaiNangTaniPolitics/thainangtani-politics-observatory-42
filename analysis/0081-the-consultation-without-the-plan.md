## 0081 – The Consultation Without the Plan

### *Thailand put 9,000MW of nuclear capacity through a public hearing at which the plan itself was never produced — and the ministry's own cost table, once read, prices a reactor at six times a gas plant*

*Last updated: 13 September 2026, 05:37:45 (ICT)*

---

## 1. Programmatic note

This node is about a procedure, not a technology. It takes no position on whether small modular reactors are safe, necessary or wise. It asks a narrower question: **on what basis was the public invited to comment?**

The occasion is the Power Development Plan for 2026–2050 (PDP2026), put to public consultation on 8 September 2026 and due before the Cabinet in November. The plan commits Thailand to **9,000MW of nuclear capacity** — a figure that did not exist in Thai energy planning three years ago. The consultation that carries it lasted one week.

What follows is drawn wherever possible from the government's own materials: the slide deck published for the hearing, the cost assumptions inside it, the Prime Minister's Office regulation that governs such consultations, and the comment thread beneath the ministry's own livestream. Where a claim rests on secondary reporting, the table marks it.

## 2. The sequence

| Date (2026) | Event |

| **9 September** | The EPPO director presents the same material at a seminar hosted by two newspapers |
| **10 September** | The same day's nuclear-board finding is reported |
| **12 September** | EGAT sets out a five-phase reactor roadmap — in a newspaper |

| **October** | To NEPO, NEPC, Cabinet |

Two features of this sequence matter. First, the **only consultation on the plan itself** — as distinct from the demand forecast underlying it — was the single day of 8 September, with seven days for written comment. Second, **substantive material continued to appear after the hearing**: the implementation roadmap for the reactors was published on 12 September, three days before the comment period closed, and not in the proceeding but in the *Bangkok Post*.

## 3. What was published

The material placed before the public was a **52-slide presentation**, distributed as a PDF in a Google Drive folder linked in a comment beneath the ministry's Facebook livestream, and advertised on a banner carrying two QR codes — one labelled *อ่านรายละเอียดแผน*, "read the details of the plan".

The folder contains that one file and nothing else.

Alongside it, a **Google form** with seven elements: four demographic questions, a section heading, and two free-text boxes — *what is your opinion of the draft* and *what questions do you have about it*. The form links to no document.

The full draft plan was not published on the EPPO media library, on the ministry's own PDP page (which still shows PDP2018 Revision 1), in the ministry's site search (which returns nothing for "PDP2026" across all six content types), on the atomic regulator's publication library (78 PDFs, none on power reactors), or on the Prime Minister's Office public-consultation portal — which is actively maintained and lists water works, canals, roads and transmission lines from the same week.

**Whether it was published anywhere at all is not a question this node can close.** What it can state is that it was not found on any of those channels, and that participants at the hearing said the same.

---

## 4. The participants said so themselves

The comment thread beneath the ministry's own livestream is the closest thing to a record of the hearing. Two participants asked, in writing and during the session, for the full draft:

> **"มีร่าง PDP ฉบับเต็มมั้ยคะ"** — *Is there a full draft of the PDP?*

> **"มีร่างแผนฉบับเต็มให้ศึกษาไหม"** — *Is there a full draft plan to study?* — followed later by: *very broad; the full draft, with the detail.*

Neither received an answer in the thread. A third participant made the comparison that a plan document is expected to contain:

> **"PDP2018 Rev.1 had a transmission development plan and a list of transmission projects with completion dates, detailed and clearly disclosed. The draft PDP2026 does not mention it."**

That comparison is verifiable, and it holds. PDP2018 Revision 1 runs to 68 pages and carries a dedicated **Chapter 5, "Transmission System Development Plan"**, from page 25. The PDP2026 deck has no equivalent.

A fourth intervention was made by **Sor Rattanamanee Polkla**, a public-interest lawyer and executive coordinator of the Community Resource Centre Foundation, who has spent two decades on cases brought by communities affected by development projects:

> **"เวทีแบบนี้ถือเป็นเวทีให้ข้อมูล ไม่อาจถือได้ว่าเป็นเวทีรับฟังความคิดเห็นเพื่อใช้ในการอนุมัติแผน"**
> *A forum of this kind is an information event. It cannot be treated as the consultation used to approve the plan.*

The ministry had, in its own words, said as much. Under the livestream it posted:

> **"การถ่ายทอดสดนี้เป็นการรับชมทางเดียว หากท่านมีคำถามหรือข้อเสนอแนะ ขอความกรุณาส่งผ่านแบบแสดงความคิดเห็นตามลิงก์ข้างต้น"**
> *This livestream is one-way viewing. If you have questions or suggestions, please submit them through the comment form linked above.*

Across the whole thread, **the ministry answered no question of substance.** Other participants reported that the Zoom link did not work; one wrote that he could not raise his hand in time; another asked whether the members of the public who had travelled to the venue and could not get in had been given somewhere appropriate to wait.

## 5. What the deck does contain — and what it prices

The deck is thin as a plan and unexpectedly informative as a document. Slide 23 carries a full capital and operating cost table for every technology in the plan. It has not, as far as this observatory can establish, been reported anywhere.

| Technology | CAPEX 2037 (THB/kW) | CAPEX 2050 (THB/kW) |
|---|---|---|
| Solar (>3MW) | **17,900** | 16,990 |
| Wind, onshore | 44,710 | 35,000 |
| Wind, offshore | 74,800 | 64,600 |
| **Geothermal** | 247,000 | **155,000** |
| Gas CCGT (700MW) | **31,450** | 40,018 |
| **SMR (300MW)** | **202,164** | **178,500** |
| Battery storage | 4,379 THB/**kWh** | 3,755 THB/**kWh** |
| CCS | 4,590 THB/tCO₂ (≈ US$135/t) | — |

Three readings follow directly.

**A reactor costs about six times a gas plant and eleven times solar**, at 2037 prices. By 2050 the gap to gas narrows to roughly four and a half times, because gas is assumed to become more expensive and the reactor cheaper.

**Geothermal becomes cheaper than nuclear by 2050** — 155,000 against 178,500 baht per kilowatt, in the same table. Geothermal is allocated **156MW** in the plan. Nuclear is allocated **9,000MW**. That ratio was raised at the hearing by a participant who had prepared the comparison in detail and who noted that he had not managed to be called on.

**The largest single item cannot be costed at all.** Every technology is priced per kilowatt. Battery storage is priced per kilowatt-*hour* — and the plan states storage only in megawatts, without discharge duration. The 14,500MW of storage in the first twelve years therefore costs anywhere between roughly 127 and 508 billion baht depending on an assumption the plan does not supply. This was the point made at the hearing by **Dusit Kruangam**, honorary president of the Thai Photovoltaic Industries Association, in a sharper form: batteries are **not a generation source and should not be counted as capacity at all**. In the plan's own 2050 capacity mix, storage is the largest single block at **31 per cent** — larger than solar.

Costed from the table, the 9,000MW of reactors come to roughly **1.6 trillion baht** — more than a third of a year's national budget. *(Own calculation: 300MW at the 2037 price plus 8,700MW at the 2050 price. The plan itself states no total.)*

## 6. What the deck does not contain

- **Where the reactors go.** Slide 48 distributes 9,000MW "across five regions". It names none. The plan's own regional framework, on slide 20, uses **seven** regions.
- **What size the units are.** The deck says "SMR **and MMR**" — small *and micro* modular reactors. At 300MW each that is thirty units; micro units are an order of magnitude smaller. The size is not fixed.
- **Who builds them.** The state utility is named for the first 300MW. For the remaining 8,700MW, secondary reporting indicates an intention to open to private investors "if the public supports it"; the deck itself is silent.
- **Where the spent fuel goes.** Thailand has no repository. At its meeting of 8 September the atomic regulator was tasked with drafting a **national policy framework** for spent fuel and waste — that is, with producing the framework, not with applying one.
- **A transmission development plan**, which the predecessor document contained.
- **Cost assumptions for green hydrogen**, which appears in the plan but not in the cost table.
- **A sensitivity analysis for CCS**, which carries exactly one cost figure.

To these the record adds a finding the deck could not contain, because it was made on the same day. On **8 September 2026** the Nuclear Energy for Peace Commission, chaired by Deputy Prime Minister **Yotchanan Wongsawat**, assessed Thailand's readiness against the IAEA's nineteen nuclear-infrastructure issues: **ten ready, six requiring development, three urgent.** The three urgent items are *setting the national policy*, *establishing the implementing organisation*, and *planning the investment finance*. The report also records that Thailand has not yet decided **where** a plant would be built or **which technology** it would use.

The country consulted on a plan committing it to 9,000MW of nuclear capacity on the same day its nuclear board recorded that the national nuclear policy had yet to be set.

## 7. The rule, and how far it reaches

Thailand has a general instrument for public consultation: the **Prime Minister's Office Regulation on Public Consultation, B.E. 2548 (2005)**. Its requirements are specific.

**Clause 7** lists eight items that must be published in advance, among them the **site**, the **effects on residents** together with preventive or remedial measures, and a **cost estimate** with its funding source. **Clause 11(2)** requires the announcement to be posted **not less than fifteen days** before the consultation begins, and also entered in the central information system maintained by the Office of the Permanent Secretary. **Clause 12** requires a **summary of results** to be published **within fifteen days** of the consultation closing. **Clause 9** distinguishes between forms, separating a *public hearing* proper from a mere *exchange of information*.

Notice for the PDP2026 hearing was given on 1 September for a hearing on 8 September: **seven days**.

But the reach of the regulation is limited by its own definition. **Clause 4** defines a *state project* as **"the carrying out of a project"** by a state agency for economic or social development, where it has wide effects on the environment, health, ways of life or local community interests. **A plan is not a project.** The eight disclosures — site, effects, cost estimate — fall due when a reactor is proposed, not when the plan committing the country to thirty or more of them is approved.

This node does not assert that the regulation was breached. It asserts something more durable: **the binding decision is taken at a level where the disclosure duties do not bite, and the disclosure duties bite at a level where the decision has already been taken.** That is the structure Sor Rattanamanee's sentence describes, and it is why no one needs to contradict her.

Consistently with this, the consultation portal that Clause 11 designates carries, from the same week, a water-supply upgrade in Nong Khai, a canal improvement in Nonthaburi, a sluice gate in Ranong, a waste-disposal site, a highway section in Khon Kaen — and a 115kV transmission line on Koh Samui. It does not carry the plan that commits the country to 9,000MW of nuclear capacity. **Under the regulation as written, it has no reason to.**

## 8. How it was reported

Coverage divided along a clean line.

Thai-language specialist media reported the substance and the friction. *Prachachat* described the hearing as ถกเข้ม — intense — and recorded that the **energy ministry's own spokesman**, Pongphon Yodmuangcharoen, observed that all four options in the draft are nearly identical for the first twelve years, so that **"the public might see no real choice"**; that he questioned CCS as a main option on cost grounds; and that he noted Thailand has **no domestic uranium**. *The Standard* carried the critique of **Praipol Koomsup**, former dean of economics at Thammasat, who argued that with inputs this incomplete it is unclear whether the plan was optimised at all, and that the four options may not be the least-cost ones.

International and trade coverage reported the announcement. A uranium-sector publication carried the hearing on 9 September under the headline "Thailand outlines nuclear power proposal at public hearing", sourced entirely to the permanent secretary via a single Thai news agency, quoting him describing PDP2026 as **"a plan for all Thais"**. It records the schedule, the 9,000MW, the tariff ceiling and the grid investment. It does not mention that the full draft was unavailable, that participants asked for it, or that the comment period was seven days. **A publication written for investors reported a consultation without noticing that there was nothing to read.**

On the supply side, one partner is on the record: **Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power** signed a memorandum of understanding with EGAT on SMR cooperation in March 2026. EGAT has said it is reviewing technologies from the United States, China, Russia and Canada. No vendor selection has been made, and none of the reporting examined for this node identifies a preferred design. *(Reported; the MoU is documented in trade press, the four-country review in the Bangkok Post of 12 September.)*

## 9. The precedent: what siting looks like when it becomes local

The open point at the end of this node — that the plan distributes 9,000MW across five unnamed regions, and that the siting question will one day move from abstract to local — is not hypothetical. Thailand has run that sequence once already, on a single research reactor, for thirty-six years. It is worth reading before the next one begins.

**Why the project exists at all.** Thailand's only research reactor, TRR-1/M1, is a 2MW TRIGA Mark III built by General Atomics, first critical on 27 October 1962 and rebuilt in 1977 to run on fuel enriched below 20 per cent. It stands at Bangkhen, **8km from Don Mueang airport**, where the international yardstick put to the cabinet asked for 14km. On **27 December 1989** the cabinet resolved to move it to Sai Mun sub-district, Ongkharak, Nakhon Nayok. Relocating the machine proved technically impossible, so a new one was to be bought. *(The 14km figure is as reported to the cabinet and recounted in the Thai press; it has not been checked against an IAEA document.)*

**What became of it.** The project was budgeted at **7,216.42 million baht** over fourteen years. General Atomics was the only bidder to pass technical review; when the procurement committee voted 5–1 to annul the tender, on the ground that the firm had no experience building large reactors, the agency's secretary-general dismissed the four members who had so voted. A contract for 3,335,574,465 baht was signed on 26 June 1997 and expired on 26 June 2001 **without construction**, because the supplier could not produce a safety certificate from the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission and no building permit could issue without one. In 2006 the Office of the Auditor-General found that **more than 1,400 million baht had been paid to the contractor with nothing built**, and recommended proceedings; the contract was terminated. The resulting claim went to **international arbitration**, and as of August 2024 the human rights commission recorded that it was still pending and that it had not been able to learn the state of it.

**The rules that applied, and how they ended.** Until 2016 the siting of research reactors was governed by a regulation of the atomic energy committee issued under the 1961 Act, published in the Gazette on **2 August 2016**. Its Table 1 set fixed radii by power class. For class (3) — above 2MW and up to 50MW, the class a 20MW reactor falls into — it required an **exclusion zone of 0.38km**, a **precautionary zone of 2.16km**, and a distance of **2.88km to the centre of any community**, "community" being defined as a concentration of more than 25,000 people.

Three days later, on 5 August 2016, the **Nuclear Energy for Peace Act B.E. 2559** was published. Its section 3 repealed the 1961 Act. Its section 2 set commencement at 180 days. Its **section 152** let regulations made under the old Act remain in force until replaced — **but for no more than 270 days** from commencement. The arithmetic is unambiguous: commencement **1 February 2017**, expiry **29 October 2017**.

The replacement ministerial regulation appeared on **9 April 2020**, 893 days later. It carries over the 320km radius for active faults. It does not carry over the table. In its place, **clause 10** provides that *the applicant shall determine the exclusion zone and the precautionary zone for the site*.

The distances were not amended, debated or replaced. They lapsed.

**What the hearings produced.** The environmental and health impact assessment for a 15–20MW reactor on 16.59 rai at Sai Mun was prepared by a consultancy and taken to two public hearings in 2022. The study area was the site plus a five-kilometre radius, containing **38,082 residents**. Attendance was **730** at the first hearing and **289** at the second — **1.92 per cent and 0.76 per cent**. A third session, on 10 September 2023, was abandoned when residents broke it up; by their own account they had decided the night before, because the format provided no way to register agreement or disagreement and they had no standing before the committees the results would go to. Over 8,000 residents had signed against the project.

**What two state bodies then found.** On **28 March 2024** the House committee on higher education, science, research and innovation heard six bodies — the citizens' association, the province, the atomic regulator, the environmental planning office, the nuclear institute and the EHIA consultancy. It resolved that the reactor was **necessary**, that the site had become urban since 1990, and that the project should be **moved to military land**; it also asked that the radioactive waste stores at Khlong Ha in Pathum Thani and at Ongkharak be relocated and tested for contamination. Its minute records that the project, begun in 1990, "cannot currently proceed".

On **6 August 2024** the **National Human Rights Commission** found two violations. The site was unsuitable: the institute's own engineering and environmental study still showed risk of radioactive release to surface water, groundwater and air, flood exposure, and an **unclear seismic risk**; and **no insurance company covers damage and compensation arising from a nuclear installation**. The consultation process was the second violation, on the attendance figures above and because the report contained no clear specification of the 20MW machine. The commission directed the ministry to put a review of the site to the **Cabinet**, the environmental planning office to have the national environment board revisit which project classes require the strictest assessment, and the regulator to supervise both waste stores with **monthly contamination testing published to the public**.

The seismic point is genuinely unresolved rather than merely contested. Two faults are mapped there, the **Ongkharak fault** and the neighbouring **Nakhon Nayok fault**. The head of geology at Chulalongkorn University announced the latter as active, related to the Mae Ping fault, 50–100km long, with geological evidence of a magnitude 6.5 event 600 to 700 years ago. The Department of Mineral Resources replied the following day that after three years of study it was **"100 per cent"** certain the Ongkharak fault is inactive and 75 per cent certain of the other. Neither position has been withdrawn.

**Where it stands.** The project is not abandoned. It is in its third attempt since 2017, now at 20MW, costed at **15,800 million baht** by the commission and 16,000 million by the objectors, on the plot chosen in 1990. And on **9–11 June 2026**, at a resort in Kanchanaburi, the atomic regulator convened 38 participants to review that same 2020 siting regulation so that it can accommodate small modular reactors, including floating ones. The agencies present included meteorology, marine administration, mineral resources, environmental planning and water resources. So did **EGAT, PTT and Global Power Synergy**. The announcement says the result will feed into the revision of the site licence framework and raise public confidence in nuclear oversight.

**And the liability question is still open in writing.** The draft *Act on Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage* (ร่างพระราชบัญญัติความรับผิดทางแพ่งต่อความเสียหายทางนิวเคลียร์) was posted on the regulator's own legal page in **September 2026**. Its statement of principle and reasons is explicit: Thailand uses nuclear energy and has a policy of preparing for small modular reactors, and although there is a law on nuclear and radiation safety supervision, **"there are as yet no specific provisions on civil liability for nuclear damage"**. Section 15 requires an operator to hold insurance **or other financial security**; section 18 creates a state Nuclear Damage Fund; section 14 removes the liability ceiling only on proof of the operator's own intent or conscious disregard. The instrument is a draft. The gap it describes is the present state of the law.

**The pattern, stated narrowly.** In 1989 a site was left because it failed a distance criterion. The site chosen instead has been through three attempts, two adverse findings by state bodies in a single year, and a hearing process that reached under one per cent of the affected population — and the numerical criteria that the first site failed no longer exist in Thai law. This node does not claim that any of this was designed. It records that the disclosure duties, the siting figures and the liability regime each arrived, lapsed or remain pending on a timetable that has never once coincided with a decision.

### Open points

**The results summary.** Clause 12 requires a summary of consultation results within fifteen days of closing — that is, by **30 September 2026**. This is the one dated, testable obligation in the entire proceeding. Whether it appears, and what it contains, will be recorded here.

**The full draft.** If the complete plan document is published — before or after Cabinet approval — §3 must be revised to say when and where. The claim made here is bounded to the channels checked.

**The five regions.** The plan distributes 9,000MW across five of its seven planning regions and identifies none. Should the allocation become public, the siting question moves from abstract to local, and this node will need a successor.

**The Ongkharak successor questions.** Whether the Cabinet acted on the commission's August 2024 direction to review the site; whether the defence ministry was ever approached about military land; what the June 2026 workshop produced and whether a draft revision exists; what became of the arbitration; whether fuel for TRR-1/M1 has been secured since the institute said in 2022 that the supply would last about five years and that a replacement needs about fifteen.

**The demand case.** The plan uses the low and medium data-centre scenarios (6,799 and 8,811MW) and discards the high case (19,808MW) "to remain consistent with the capacity to develop infrastructure and the transmission system". Whether demand is being planned for or planned around is a separate question, treated in the data-centre material of this observatory.
