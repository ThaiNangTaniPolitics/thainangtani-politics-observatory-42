## 0035 – ISOC's Mandate: Law, Strategy, Programmes
### *What the Internal Security Act, ISOC's own strategy and its mass programmes say — and what they do not*

*Last updated: 9 October 2026, 04:20:00 (ICT)*

The Internal Security Operations Command (ISOC, กอ.รมน.) is described in this archive as the back-end of Thailand's dual governance (0029–0037). This node sets out what the documents say about ISOC's ideological mandate: the statute, ISOC's own strategy, its mass programmes, and the scholarly account of their origins. It separates what is documented from what is interpretation.

*Correction (October 2026): an earlier version of this node carried no sources and attributed to the Internal Security Act an "operational immunity" and an "insulation from administrative courts" that the Act does not contain in that form (Section 5). It has been rebuilt from the sources listed below.*

-----

## 1. Scope and Method

Evidence levels used in this node:

- **Level 1** — primary document read: the Royal Gazette text of the Act, ISOC's own strategy and course announcements, platform disclosures.
- **Level 2** — scholarly or reputable secondary source read. The main source is Puangthong Pawakapan, *Infiltrating Society: The Thai Military's Internal Security Affairs* (ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute, 2021), read in the original; page numbers are the printed pages.
- **Level 4** — press reports read, primary document not obtained.

Three statements in Puangthong are not adopted here because the statute does not carry them or they are internally inconsistent: that the 2008 Act "grants ISOC officers impunity" (p. 57; no section is named, and the Act contains no such clause, see Section 5); the reference to "section 16(10)" (p. 57; section 16 has four items); and the dating of ISOC's membership table to "March 2012" in its heading and "March 2015" in the text (p. 128). On p. 55 the December 2007 election is associated with Yingluck Shinawatra; she became prime minister after the 2011 election.

-----

## 2. Origins of the Doctrine: The Prime Ministerial Orders of the 1980s

ISOC's authority rested on administrative orders until 2008 (Puangthong, p. 55). The decisive orders were issued under General Prem Tinsulanonda as prime minister (March 1980–August 1988):

- **Order No. 66/2523** (1980) and **Order No. 65/2525** (1982) replaced the hardline military offensive against the Communist Party of Thailand with a "political offensive". Puangthong: *"The two orders required all state agencies at all levels to comply with their implementation. ISOC was responsible for policy implementation, issuing directives, and coordinating the work of ministries, bureaus, departments and organizations"* (p. 43). And: *"With his two orders Prem created a unified system for internal security activities under ISOC, and ordered all state agencies to comply with their directives as a matter of national priority, thus granting ISOC unprecedented authority"* (p. 44).
- **Order No. 83/2526** (1983) placed civilian, military and police agencies under ISOC's command, together with mass organisations previously under other ministries, such as the Volunteer Defence Corps (p. 43, citing Ball and Mathieson 2007).
- **Order No. 47/2529** (March 1986) reaffirmed the two orders and, in Puangthong's summary, directed all government agencies *"to fully participate in promoting the democratic regime with the monarch as the head of the state"* (pp. 43–44, citing Suchit 1987).

These orders are the documented origin of the link between internal security, mass organisation and the institutions of nation, religion and monarchy that the 2008 Act later codified (Level 2).

-----

## 3. The Statutory Mandate: Internal Security Act B.E. 2551 (2008)

**Origin.** After the coup of 19 September 2006, the junta leader appointed General Surayud Chulanont, *"a retired army commander and a member of King Bhumibol's Privy Council, as prime minister"*; the Act was *"a top priority of the Surayud administration"* (Puangthong, p. 54). It was passed by the junta-appointed National Legislative Assembly on 20 December 2007 and came into force on 28 February 2008 (pp. 54–55; Royal Gazette Vol. 125, Part 39 Kor, 27 February 2008, pp. 33–43). The Prime Minister is ISOC's director; the Army Commander is deputy director (§ 5).

**The mandate.** Section 7 lists ISOC's duties. Item 4 reads (Royal Gazette, p. 36; Level 1):

> (๔) เสริมสร้างให้ประชาชนตระหนักในหน้าที่ที่ต้องพิทักษ์รักษาไว้ซึ่งชาติ ศาสนา และพระมหากษัตริย์ สร้างความรักความสามัคคีของคนในชาติ รวมทั้งส่งเสริมให้ประชาชนเข้ามามีส่วนร่วมในการป้องกัน และแก้ไขปัญหาต่าง ๆ ที่กระทบต่อความมั่นคงภายในราชอาณาจักรและความสงบเรียบร้อยของสังคม

*(4) To strengthen the public's awareness of its duty to protect and preserve the nation, religion and the monarchy; to build love and unity among the people of the nation; and to promote public participation in preventing and solving problems that affect the internal security of the Kingdom and public order.*

Two points follow from the wording:

- **The reference to the monarchy is in the statute itself.** It is not an inference from ISOC's practice.
- **The duty is framed as public awareness.** The Act charges ISOC with strengthening the *population's* sense of its duty. It does not charge ISOC with protecting the monarchy directly. Puangthong reads § 7(4) as a mandate for mass work: *"This section unequivocally refers to ISOC's activities on mass mobilization and organization"* (p. 57).

Section 7(4) was not amended by the order of the head of the National Council for Peace and Order No. 51/2560 (November 2017), which amended other parts of the Act (parliamentary synopsis of the consolidated text; the order itself not read).

## 4. ISOC's Own Strategy, 2017–2021

ISOC's published strategy for B.E. 2560–2564 (2017–2021) is more explicit than the Act (Level 1; printed page numbers):

- **Vision** (p. 29) — no reference to the monarchy. In translation: ISOC is *"the main organisation for integrating, directing and coordinating the maintenance of internal security, for the security of the state and the peace of the people."*
- **Mission 1.2** (p. 29):

> ๑.2 ปกป้องสถาบันหลักของชาติและสร้างความรักสามัคคีของคนในชาติด้วยพลังประชารัฐ ภายใต้การปกครองระบอบประชาธิปไตยอันมีพระมหากษัตริย์ทรงเป็นประมุข

*1.2 To protect the main institutions of the nation and build love and unity among the people through the power of state and people, under the democratic form of government with the King as Head of State.*

- **Basic objective 3.2** (p. 29):

> ๓.2 การบูรณาการและประสานงานกับทุกภาคส่วนในการพิทักษ์รักษาสถาบันชาติ ศาสนา และพระมหากษัตริย์ ตลอดจนการปกครองในระบอบประชาธิปไตยอันมีพระมหากษัตริย์ทรงเป็นประมุข

*3.2 Integration and coordination with all sectors in protecting the institutions of nation, religion and monarchy, and the democratic form of government with the King as Head of State.*

- **Strategic issue 3** (pp. 34–35) is devoted to *"strengthening the security of the main institutions of the nation"*; its goal 1 is *"loyalty, love and unity, and joint protection of the main institutions of the nation"*, and strategy 2.2 is *"to promote learning about the importance of the monarchy in Thai national history"*.
- **Threat assessment** (p. 25). The first threat listed is *"การล่วงละเมิดสถาบันพระมหากษัตริย์"* — *"violations against the institution of the monarchy"*. The strategy states that the trend *"persists and tends to intensify continuously, both domestically and abroad"*.

Unlike the Act, the strategy assigns protection of the main institutions to ISOC itself (Mission 1.2) and ranks violations against the monarchy as the first threat to internal security. The current strategy (from 2022) could not be obtained; ISOC's website was not accessible.

## 5. Legal Shielding: What the Act Provides and What It Does Not

The earlier version of this node, and nodes 0029, 0033 and 0037, describe the Act as granting ISOC "operational immunity" and "insulation from administrative courts". The text of the Act supports neither formula as stated (Level 1):

- **No immunity clause.** Sections 1–26 contain no exemption from civil, criminal or disciplinary liability. Section 20 provides the opposite: where the exercise of ISOC's powers under section 16(1) causes loss to an innocent person, *"ISOC shall arrange for that person to receive compensation"* (unofficial ICJ translation, checked against the parliamentary synopsis). The director sues and can be sued in the name of the Office of the Prime Minister (§ 5).
- **A redirection of jurisdiction, limited to Chapter 2.** Section 23 (Royal Gazette, pp. 42–43):

> มาตรา ๒๓ บรรดาข้อกำหนด ประกาศ คำสั่ง หรือการกระทำตามหมวดนี้ไม่อยู่ในบังคับของกฎหมายว่าด้วยวิธีปฏิบัติราชการทางปกครอง
> การดำเนินคดีใด ๆ อันเนื่องมาจากข้อกำหนด ประกาศ คำสั่ง หรือการกระทำตามหมวดนี้ให้อยู่ในอำนาจของศาลยุติธรรม …

*Section 23. Regulations, announcements, orders or acts under this Chapter are not subject to the law on administrative procedure. Any case arising from regulations, announcements, orders or acts under this Chapter falls within the jurisdiction of the Courts of Justice …*

Cases are thus heard by the ordinary courts rather than the administrative courts. This applies only to acts "under this Chapter" — Chapter 2, the special powers that require a cabinet resolution under section 15 for a defined area and period. ISOC's standing duties under section 7 are not covered.

- **Where the immunity actually is.** The Emergency Decree B.E. 2548 (2005) contains both elements the old formula attributed to the Act. Its section 16 excludes the law on administrative procedure *and* the law establishing the administrative courts. Its section 17 provides that officials *"ไม่ต้องรับผิดทั้งทางแพ่ง ทางอาญา หรือทางวินัย"* — *"shall not be liable civilly, criminally or disciplinarily"* — for acts in good faith, without discrimination and not beyond what is necessary (Royal Gazette Vol. 122, Part 58 Kor, 16 July 2005, p. 8).

The shielding of ISOC's operations is therefore a matter of the Emergency Decree where it is in force, and of section 23 for Chapter 2 measures. It is not a general feature of the 2008 Act.

## 6. Mass Organisations and Programmes

**Documented in ISOC's own material (Level 1).** ISOC's announcement for the Thai National Defence Volunteers course (ทสปช.) in fiscal year 2023 admits participants from age 17, makes them part of *"ISOC's mass network"*, and sets as a condition of entry:

> 3.4 เป็นผู้เลื่อมใสการปกครองในระบอบประชาธิปไตยอันมีพระมหากษัตริย์ทรงเป็นประมุข

*3.4 Being a devotee of the democratic form of government with the King as Head of State.*

The course content includes *"strengthening knowledge and understanding of the main institutions of the nation"*. The 2017–2021 strategy plans *"effective people's news networks"* and counts their number as an indicator (p. 33).

**Documented by Puangthong (Level 2).**

- **Purpose of the revival since 2006** — her analysis: *"The foremost objective of the revival of mass organizations and mobilization since the coup in 2006 is to protect the monarchy, the royal hegemony and status quo of the establishment by strengthening and expanding the network of royalist citizens"* (p. 120). This is her conclusion, drawn from the documents she cites.
- **Programmes** (pp. 121–128): training manuals produced with Kasetsart University (2010); memoranda with the Office of the Private Education Commission and the non-formal education office (2017); the territorial defence student curriculum; the "Diamonds in the Mud" scholarships (since 1984).
- **Size**: ISOC's organised mass, by her count from ISOC's website, at 236,419 members (p. 128, date stated inconsistently, see Section 1).
- **Effect**: by her assessment, the programmes have had the least effect on young people (p. 142).

## 7. Information Operations: A Delimitation

Two cases are often cited in this context. Neither concerns the monarchy as described in Sections 3–6:

- The network of 926 Twitter accounts disclosed in October 2020 was attributed by the platform to the **Royal Thai Army**: *"a network of accounts … that we can reliably link to the Royal Thai Army (RTA)"* (Level 1). The Stanford Internet Observatory report on the network does not mention ISOC.
- The **Pulony** website attacked human-rights defenders in the Deep South. According to press reports, an appeal court held ISOC responsible on 11 June 2026 (Level 4; judgment and finality not verified).

ISOC's strategy assigns responsibility for information operations to its Office of Security Policy and Strategy (สำนักนโยบายและยุทธศาสตร์ความมั่นคง, item 2.3.4, p. 4).

-----

## 8. Findings and Limits

**Documented:**
- The 2008 Act charges ISOC with strengthening public awareness of the duty to protect nation, religion and monarchy (§ 7(4)).
- ISOC's 2017–2021 strategy goes further. It assigns ISOC the protection of the main institutions (Mission 1.2) and lists violations against the monarchy as the first threat to internal security.
- ISOC's mass programmes make devotion to the form of government with the King as Head of State a condition of entry.
- The Act contains no immunity clause. Its jurisdiction rule is limited to Chapter 2 measures.

**Interpretation:** that this mandate forms the ideological core of the "back-end" described in 0037. The term "network monarchy" (McCargo, *The Pacific Review* 18(4), 2005, pp. 499–519; not consulted) is used by Puangthong as one of several frames (p. 6). It is not used in ISOC's documents and is not adopted here.

**Open:** the text of NCPO order 51/2560; ISOC's strategy from 2022; the appeal judgment in the Pulony case; ISOC's 2012 publication that Puangthong cites for the priority of mass organisations (p. 121).
