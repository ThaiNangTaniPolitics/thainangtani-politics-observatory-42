## 0064 – The Traceable Ballot

### *Vote secrecy, the 2026 barcode system, and the prosecution of those who proved the breach*

*Last updated: 29 August 2026 (ICT)*

---

On 8 February 2026 Thailand held a general election in which every ballot paper carried a code
unique to that single sheet. The code corresponds to a running number on the counterfoil, and the
counterfoil carries the voter's name and signature or fingerprint. The chain from mark to person
is therefore complete on the face of the material itself.

The Election Commission does not dispute that the chain exists. Its defence is that the two ends
are kept apart. That defence is the subject of this node, and it fails — not because the
Commission is presumed to act badly, but because a secret ballot cannot rest on the promise of
the body that holds both halves.

Two further features make the case unusual. The codes appeared on no specimen ballot published
before the vote, so the voter met them for the first time inside the booth. And the citizens who
photographed and decoded them, in order to demonstrate the flaw, were pursued for sedition. The
breach is thus real on the documents and, by the architecture around it, difficult to prove from
outside.

-----

## I. What changed

Codes on Thai ballots are not new. In 2024 and earlier they denoted the **booklet**: identical
throughout a book of ballots, useful for distribution control and worthless for identifying an
individual sheet.

In 2026 the code became **unique to each ballot**, with a running number matching the serial on
the counterfoil. Thai PBS Verify sets out the distribution: a **barcode on the party-list (pink)
ballots**, a **QR code on the constituency (green) ballots**, each carrying its own running
number. The referendum ballot (yellow) carried neither.

That single change — from batch marker to individual marker — is the whole of the matter. Every
subsequent question follows from it.

## II. The identification chain

The chain is short and each link is documented: the code on the ballot leads to the serial on the
counterfoil, the counterfoil to the voter number, the voter number to the voter list, and the
list to the person.

Thai PBS Verify has established that clause 184 of the EC regulations places counterfoils
**inside the same ballot box** as the papers themselves, and that the counterfoils carry matching
numbers together with voters' names and signatures. The link is therefore not hypothetical: it is
the normal condition of the retained material.

## III. The specimen ballots

The codes were absent from the template ballots the Commission released before polling day.
ANFREL, the Asian network for election observation, recorded on 17 February 2026 that the codes
"were not included on template ballot papers released before the elections" — voters had no
advance notice of them.

This is the point at which the case ceases to depend on technical argument. Whether a given code
can in fact be resolved to a person, whether one or both ballot types are affected, whether the
Commission's storage arrangements hold — none of it needs to be decided if the voter encountered
the mark for the first time in the booth, on a paper whose published specimen did not show it.

The standard for that situation already exists in Thai constitutional practice. In 2006 the
Constitutional Court annulled a general election, decision 9/2549, **partly** because the polling
booths were arranged so that voting could be observed. The question there was not whether anyone
had in fact watched; it was the conditions under which the vote was cast. Applied here, the
question is not whether anyone can in fact be identified, but whether the voter had to assume it
was possible while marking the ballot.

*(No claim is made here about how people actually voted. A chilling effect of this kind cannot be
measured, and the argument does not require it: the condition is sufficient.)*

## IV. Stated rationale against actual effect

The codes were authorised under the EC's regulations on the election of MPs and introduced, on
the Commission's account, without prior notice, as an anti-counterfeiting and distribution
measure: to detect over-printing, verify the twenty-per-booklet count, prevent floating ballots
and assign responsibility if papers went astray.

Each of those purposes is real, and none of them requires a code unique to the individual sheet.
**Authentication is not identification.** A ballot is protected against forgery by watermarks,
microprint and controlled paper stock; a batch is controlled by a batch number. Uniqueness serves
one function that batch marking cannot: telling one ballot from another. That is the capability
at issue, and it is the one the stated rationale does not need.

## V. The Commission's shifting account

On 13 February the Commission stated that the barcode identified the booklet and not the voter's
choice. On 18 February it acknowledged that serial numbers and barcodes could be used to trace a
voter's ballot paper.

Both statements are on the record within five days of each other. The second is the one that
governs, and it concedes the capability that the first denied.

## VI. Why "stored separately" does not cure the breach

The Election Commission's principal defence is that ballots and counterfoils are kept in separate
storage, so the identification chain cannot be completed. This does not meet the constitutional
standard. A secret ballot is guaranteed not by a custodian's promise to keep two records apart,
but by the **impossibility of reconstructing the link between voter and vote**. Security that
depends on trusting the record-holder is not a security property; it is a courtesy. The defence
fails on four independent grounds, each sufficient on its own.

**Reversibility.** Separate storage can be recombined. A barrier that the party holding both
halves is free to remove is not a guarantee — it is a postponement.

**The digitisation gap.** Physical separation is meaningless if the ballot–counterfoil
correspondence was captured in digital form at any point before the records were separated:
during printing, distribution control, the count, or the authentication process. The Commission
has not disclosed whether, or at what stage, such a record was created. Its own stated rationale
— detecting over-printing, verifying booklet completeness, assigning accountability if ballots
leak — describes precisely the kind of tracking system that would generate one. The burden is on
the Commission to show that no digital link exists; it has not discharged it. *(That such a
record was created is not asserted here. What is asserted is that the question is unanswered and
that the answer lies with the Commission.)*

**Mutability over time.** A storage arrangement maintained today guarantees nothing tomorrow.
Records that exist can be linked later, under a different administration and for a different
purpose. The secrecy of a vote cast in 2026 cannot depend on the future discretion of every
future custodian.

**Absence of independent verification.** The separation is self-attested by the Commission — the
same body whose conduct is under challenge — and is subject to no public or independent audit.
Where the sole assurance is trust in the custodian, and where citizens who attempted to verify
the codes have themselves been prosecuted, the claim is unfalsifiable from outside. An
unfalsifiable assurance is not evidence of secrecy; it is its absence.

Taken together, these grounds move the secret ballot from a **design property**, which can be
verified, to a **custodial promise**, which cannot. A constitutional guarantee of secret suffrage
cannot rest on the continued goodwill of the authority that designed the traceable system in the
first place. The proper question is not whether the link has been exploited, but whether it can
be — and on the Commission's own account, it can.

## VII. Not repairable by a clean count

The breach, if the court finds one, occurred at the moment of voting rather than at the count. A
recount conducted faultlessly would confirm what was marked on the papers; it cannot restore the
conditions under which the marks were made. This is why the remedies ordinarily available in
election disputes do not fit the case, and why the argument over annulment is not a
disproportionate response to a technicality.

## VIII. The legal tracks

Three proceedings run in parallel, and they are frequently confused with one another.

**Before the Constitutional Court**, twenty-two complaints reached the Ombudsman, who referred
the matter onward; the court accepted it on 18 March 2026 by six votes to three and has set its
ruling for 28 September. The petition invokes the constitutional guarantee of secret suffrage.
The route matters: under section 231 the Ombudsman refers a **provision of law** whose
constitutionality is in question to the Constitutional Court, while a **rule, order or other act**
of an agency goes to the Administrative Court. That this case sits with the Constitutional Court
implies it was classified as review of a provision, not of the act of printing ballots. The
referral document has not been published; until it is, the safe formulation is that the Ombudsman
referred the matter to the Constitutional Court.

**A separate barcode petition has already been dismissed, and the manner of its dismissal is
instructive.** On 2 July 2026 the Constitutional Court unanimously rejected three petitions, the
first of them brought by **Natthida Nikrothangkun** against the Election Commission over the
decision to print barcodes on party-list ballots; she argued that barcodes, used in place of the
QR codes originally budgeted for, compromised ballot secrecy. The court held that she had not
shown that her own rights or freedoms were directly infringed, that her petition expressed
personal opinions, and that it did not explain how the use of barcodes had undermined the secrecy
of the vote. It therefore failed the requirements for a constitutional complaint **under section
213**.

Two things follow. First, the two section numbers that appear in reporting belong to two
different proceedings rather than to one inconsistency: section 213 was the route of the
individual complainant, dismissed in July on admissibility; section 231 is the Ombudsman's route,
which is what will be decided on 28 September. Second, the July dismissal is not an indication of
the September outcome, because it turned on a threshold that does not arise in the Ombudsman's
case: there the standing is the Ombudsman's, not an individual voter's.

The reasoning nonetheless deserves recording. The complainant was faulted, among other things,
for not explaining how the codes undermined the secrecy of the vote. Supplying that explanation
is precisely what the six people described in section IX set out to do — and what they now face a
criminal complaint for. The court's admissibility standard and the Commission's complaint point
in opposite directions at the same act.

**Before the Administrative Court**, two People's Party candidates have brought proceedings
concerning the handling of the data.

**Before the Criminal Court for Corruption and Misconduct Cases**, the People's Party has lodged a
complaint under section 157 against the Commission and its secretary-general.

An acceptance by six votes to three is a decision to hear the case. It is not an indication of
the outcome, and nothing in this node predicts one.

## IX. The proof is prosecuted

At the re-run of the general election on 22 February 2026, at polling station 9 in Bangkok's
Khannayao district, six people photographed ballots and counterfoils and attempted to decode the
codes on both the constituency and the party-list papers. The Election Commission lodged a
criminal complaint against them with the Crime Suppression Division. It has not published the
complaint itself.

The six are not anonymous. According to Khaosod English of 28 February 2026 they are **Somchai
Srisutthiyakorn**, a former Election Commissioner; **Parit Wacharasindhu**, a spokesman for the
People's Party; **Thammathir Sukchotirat**, an academic working on technology; **Thanarat
Kuwattanapan**, a blockchain entrepreneur; **Chaipan Chawalawanichai**, an online commentator;
and **Songpol Ruangsamut**, chief photographer at the news outlet Spacebar. The first of them is
the same former commissioner whose technical account appears in the fact-check cited in
section I.

The offences alleged are serious and are drawn from five different provisions: membership of an
illegal secret organisation under section 209 of the Criminal Code, carrying up to ten years for
organisers; sedition or incitement under section 116, up to seven years; obstruction of the
Commission's duties; the importing or dissemination of false information affecting national
security or public order under section 14 of the Computer Crime Act; and the unlawful opening of
sealed documents, reported under section 322.

The Commission's position is that voting under the 2017 constitution must be direct and secret,
that any attempt to establish how an individual voted would itself be electoral fraud, and that
cameras placed near the polling station deterred some voters. It rejects the charge that it is
pursuing ordinary citizens or the press: more than a hundred members of the public and of the
media observed the vote without consequence, and the complaint is directed only at persons it
believes acted in coordination and with dishonest intent.

That position is on the record and belongs in any account of the case. So does its effect. The
People's Party has described the complaint as a strategic lawsuit against public participation;
iLaw's Yingcheep Atchanont and the activist Sombat Boonngam-anong have called it
disproportionate; Spacebar calls the action against its photographer intimidation of journalists
performing their work; and the Thai Journalists Association has asked the Commission to
reconsider, warning of a chilling effect on the reporting of elections.

The consequence is structural rather than rhetorical. The evidentiary bar in a secrecy case is
proof that the link between ballot and voter can be resolved. Producing that proof requires doing
what these six did. If that conduct attracts complaints under five criminal provisions, the
standard becomes one that can be met only at a cost few will accept — and the resulting absence
of proof is then available to the authority as a defence. The same loop appears elsewhere in this
archive (see 0062): the architecture prosecutes its documenters.

*Two limits on this section. A criminal complaint is not a charge, and none of these allegations
has been tested in court; nothing here says whether the conduct was lawful. And the demonstration
took place at a re-run poll two weeks after the general election. That the codes were still
present on both ballot types on 22 February is documented; whether the papers were otherwise
identical to those used on 8 February is not, and the point should not be pressed beyond what the
sources carry.*

## X. The administrator, removed by spreadsheet

In June 2026 the Commission's secretary-general, **Sawaeng Boonmee** — the named defendant in the
section 157 complaint and the official who administered the barcode election and moved against the
six decoders — faced dismissal. The trigger was neither the barcode breach nor the Senate
collusion case in which he is co-accused, but an annual **performance score**: under section 56 of
the organic law on the Election Commission the secretary-general must reach sixty per cent, and he
reportedly fell short.

On the documentary record he is the executor common to every contested national vote of the era:
the 2019 general election and its seat-formula dispute, the 2023 election and the blocking of its
winner, the 2024 Senate self-selection, and the 2026 barcode election. *(This is a documented
role, not a claim that he personally caused each outcome.)*

The evaluation itself is contested. Four of the commissioners who scored him are reported to have
done so after leaving office, although the rules require serving commissioners; the body that
polices electoral procedure did not run its own procedure cleanly. Why the removal came when it
did is not established, and this node does not settle it. Several readings are available and each
is falsifiable — that the score pre-positions an operational fall guy in the collusion case, in
which the same people are co-accused; that it weakens a potential witness; that it was executed
before the outgoing board lost standing; that the post itself is worth controlling. The benign
reading, genuine poor performance sloppily executed, does not account for the fresh legal risk the
four accepted by voting without authority.

None of this makes Sawaeng a victim: he is co-accused in the substance. The point is narrower and
harder. **A performance score is the wrong instrument for the right problem.** The substantive
reckoning — the section 157 complaint, the constitutional petition, the collusion proceedings — is
displaced by an internal metric, one official is offered up, and the institution continues.

## XI. The ruling as an open data point

The court that will decide whether the ballot was secret is itself one of the independent bodies
whose independence the wider argument of this archive puts in question — and the opposition leader
has said as much publicly, noting that the judges were selected through a Senate whose own
selection is under investigation for collusion. The ruling is therefore a test of whether an
accountability layer functions, and its value as evidence lies in the reasoning as much as the
result.

Two counter-readings deserve to be recorded before the date, so that neither can be adopted
afterwards to fit the outcome.

The first holds that the political consequence is small. Stithorn Thananithichot of Chulalongkorn
University argues that the secrecy problem is confined to the party-list ballots, that the
constituency QR code cannot be traced to a voter, and that a fresh party-list election alone would
not change the balance of power; he attributes the affair to the Commission's failure to
understand the technology rather than to partisanship. Two other sources — Thai PBS Verify and the
constitutional-law commentary at I·CONnect — place a running number on **both** ballot types,
which is the premise his reading depends on.

The second is the possibility that a dismissal is itself the manoeuvre: a finding of "not proven"
in a case where the proof has been criminalised.

What should be recorded on 28 September is therefore two things, kept apart: the outcome, and the
reasoning adopted to reach it.

## XII. Synthesis

A traceable ballot applies to the franchise the logic this archive documents elsewhere in the
security and administrative apparatus: the capacity to identify is built first, and the assurance
that it will not be used is offered afterwards, by the body that built it.

The form of the election was preserved in full — parties, campaigns, polling stations, a count.
The function that makes the form worth preserving is what the codes placed in the custody of an
administrator. Whether that custody was ever abused is unknown and, for the moment, unknowable
from outside. That is the condition the constitution's guarantee of secret suffrage was written to
prevent.
